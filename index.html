FIELD NOTES

Every pixel is a light ray traced backwards from the camera through the curved spacetime of a spinning (Kerr) black hole. At spin 0 this is the non-rotating Schwarzschild case. Distances are in Schwarzschild radii rs = 2GM/c², time in rs/c. The numbers below are for spin 0; the spin section says what changes.

01Event horizon · r = 1 rs

The surface of no return. Rays that cross it never reach the camera, so it renders as pure black. The dark “shadow” you see is larger, about 2.6 rs across in radius, because light is captured well before it reaches the horizon.

02Photon sphere · 1.5 rs

Here gravity is strong enough for light to orbit in a circle. Rays that skim it wrap around the hole and pile up into the thin bright photon ring at the edge of the shadow.

03Gravitational lensing

The far side of the disk is bent up and over the shadow, forming the arching halo. Fainter, thinner copies appear closer in; each is light that circled the hole one more time. Set Lensing to 0% to see the same scene with gravity switched off for light.

04Accretion disk & ISCO · 3 rs

Gas orbits at the Keplerian rate Ω = √(GM/r³), so the inner disk spins faster. Inside the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) no stable orbit exists and gas plunges in, so the disk has an inner edge.

05Spin · Kerr black holes

Real black holes spin. Spin drags spacetime around with it, and the effect gets stronger close in. Inside the ergosphere (out to 1 rs at the equator) nothing can stay still relative to the distant stars; a static camera there is swept along, shown as ERGO in the telemetry. Spin shrinks the horizon (r₊ = 0.53 rs at a/M = 0.998) and lets gas orbiting with the spin come much closer: the ISCO moves from 3 rs down to 0.62 rs. Light moving with the rotation can skim closer than light moving against it, so the shadow is flattened on the side where the hole turns toward you. That is the "D" shape. Turn on the grid background and slide Spin to watch it form.

06Thick, glowing disk

Real accretion flows have depth. Hot, inefficient ones like the gas around M87* puff up into a thick, mostly transparent torus. The Thick disk model traces each ray through that glowing gas instead of stopping at a sheet: it adds up light along the way and lets some of the background through. Gas inside the ISCO keeps glowing faintly as it plunges toward the horizon. This produces the hazy ring of the Event Horizon Telescope images, where the shadow is a dark dip in the glow rather than a hard black disk. Try EHT view (key 5) with a thickness of 0.3.

07Relativistic jets · press J

Spinning holes threaded by magnetic fields launch twin jets of plasma along the spin axis at close to the speed of light. Their power comes from the hole's rotation (the Blandford–Znajek process, P ∝ a²), so at spin 0 there are no jets. The jet coming toward you is Doppler-boosted and blazes; the one going away is dimmed, which is why the counter-jet of M87 is so faint. The jet behind the hole is lensed into view like everything else. Bright knots ride outward, and on the approaching side they can appear to move faster than light, an effect of light-travel time.

08Doppler beaming

Gas near the ISCO moves at about half the speed of light. The side moving toward you looks brighter and bluer (intensity ∝ g⁴), the receding side dimmer and redder. Try the Blackbody palette at 100% Doppler.

09Gravitational redshift & time dilation

Light climbing out of the well loses energy by √(1 − rs/r). Clocks near the hole run slow by the same factor. The telemetry shows your camera's clock rate and the orbiting probe's (√(1 − 1.5 rs/r) for a circular orbit).

10Falling in · press D

Released from its orbit, the probe follows a real free-fall path. Its own clock (PROBE τ) reaches the horizon in a finite time. Seen from outside, it slows, reddens and fades, and never quite crosses: its light takes ever longer to climb out. Tidal forces stretch it along the fall direction. The TIDAL · 2 m readout gives the real stretching force on a 2 m body. It is lethal near a 10 M☉ black hole, but barely noticeable at the horizon of a supermassive one.

11Light echoes · press X

Light that takes the long way round arrives late. Flash sets off a short flare in the gas behind the hole. You first see it lifted over the top of the shadow, then a few rs/c later again below it, and much later and fainter in the photon ring, each copy from light that circled the hole once more. Flashing slows time to ×0.25 so the echoes are easy to follow. The disk you see is always the disk as it was when its light left: lensed images show slightly older gas. The ray inspector gives each pixel's light travel time and how much longer it took than a straight line.

12Ray inspector · press T

Click any pixel to follow its light ray backwards. A light ray around a non-rotating black hole always stays in one plane, so the diagram shows its whole path. It tells you whether the ray hit the disk (and which image: primary, secondary, or deeper in the photon ring), the probe or the sky, or fell into the horizon. The deciding number is the impact parameter b: rays with b below b꜀ ≈ 2.6 rs are captured.

13Observer modes · press O

What you see depends on how you move. A static camera hovers in place. An orbiting one rides a circular orbit, at 0.5c at the ISCO. A free-falling one drops from rest far away and reaches about 0.88c near the horizon. Boost moves toward the view at a speed you choose. Motion causes aberration: light from ahead bunches into a smaller patch of sky, so the hole looks smaller and farther away, and the forward view is brighter and bluer (intensity ∝ D⁴). A falling observer near the horizon sees a much smaller shadow than one hovering at the same radius.

14Mass scale & Hawking radiation

Quantum effects make every black hole glow faintly, at the Hawking temperature T = ħc³/8πGMkB. Smaller holes are hotter. A 10¹² kg primordial hole is about 10¹¹ K. A stellar one is a few billionths of a kelvin, colder than the cosmic microwave background, so it absorbs more than it radiates and slowly grows. Evaporation time scales as M³. Otherwise the picture is scale-free: it is identical for a 10 M☉ black hole and M87*, but one rs/c is 0.1 ms for the first and 18 hours for the second.
