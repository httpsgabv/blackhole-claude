FIELD NOTES

Every pixel is a light ray traced backwards from the camera through curved spacetime around a non-rotating (Schwarzschild) black hole. Distances are in Schwarzschild radii rs = 2GM/c², time in rs/c.

01Event horizon · r = 1 rs

The surface of no return. Rays that cross it never reach the camera, so it renders as pure black. The dark “shadow” you see is larger, about 2.6 rs across in radius, because light is captured well before it reaches the horizon.

02Photon sphere · 1.5 rs

Here gravity is strong enough for light to orbit in a circle. Rays that skim it wrap around the hole and pile up into the thin bright photon ring at the edge of the shadow.

03Gravitational lensing

The far side of the disk is bent up and over the shadow, forming the arching halo. Fainter, thinner copies appear closer in; each is light that circled the hole one more time. Set Lensing to 0% to see the same scene with gravity switched off for light.

04Accretion disk & ISCO · 3 rs

Gas orbits at the Keplerian rate Ω = √(GM/r³), so the inner disk spins faster. Inside the innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) no stable orbit exists and gas plunges in, so the disk has an inner edge.

05Doppler beaming

Gas near the ISCO moves at about half the speed of light. The side moving toward you looks brighter and bluer (intensity ∝ g⁴), the receding side dimmer and redder. Try the Blackbody palette at 100% Doppler.

06Gravitational redshift & time dilation

Light climbing out of the well loses energy by √(1 − rs/r). Clocks near the hole run slow by the same factor. The telemetry shows your camera's clock rate and the orbiting probe's (√(1 − 1.5 rs/r) for a circular orbit).

07Falling in · press D

Released from its orbit, the probe follows a real free-fall path. Its own clock (PROBE τ) reaches the horizon in a finite time. Seen from outside, it slows, reddens and fades, and never quite crosses: its light takes ever longer to climb out. Tidal forces stretch it along the fall direction. The TIDAL · 2 m readout gives the real stretching force on a 2 m body. It is lethal near a 10 M☉ black hole, but barely noticeable at the horizon of a supermassive one.

08Ray inspector · press T

Click any pixel to follow its light ray backwards. A light ray around a non-rotating black hole always stays in one plane, so the diagram shows its whole path. It tells you whether the ray hit the disk (and which image: primary, secondary, or deeper in the photon ring), the probe or the sky, or fell into the horizon. The deciding number is the impact parameter b: rays with b below b꜀ ≈ 2.6 rs are captured.

09Mass scale

Everything scales with mass. The picture is identical for a 10 M☉ black hole and M87*, but one rs/c is 0.1 ms for the first and 18 hours for the second.
